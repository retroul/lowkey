    how a tool gets in

    Every pick comes from reading the project’s own docs, published audits and issue tracker. Nobody paid to be here or to be left off. Every category also says what it looks for, right above its list.

    • Encryption

      If the company can read your data, assume it eventually will. Tools that hold your stuff should only ever see it scrambled.

    • Source

      Open source wins ties. It doesn’t make a tool safe, but it means people outside the company can check.

    • Money

      We look for a business model you can see: you pay, donors pay, or it’s free software. Free with no visible income usually means you’re what’s for sale.

    • Upkeep

      A release in the last year and a public history of fixing security bugs. Abandoned tools come off, even good ones.

    • Record

      When police or hackers showed up, was there anything to take? A company caught keeping data it promised not to keep gets cut.

    • Setup

      Labelled honestly. Easy means install and go. Advanced means you’ll read documentation and probably break something once.

    Tools change owners, policies and code. Before you trust one with anything that matters, read its current privacy policy on its own site.
